# Product Process

Rules for how to approach discovery, user research, and design decisions. These are moderately-to-strongly held.

## Invest in Discovery Upfront

Insufficient discovery before development leads to products that reflect the builder's assumptions rather than the user's mental model.

1. Before naming anything, structuring any workflow, or designing any interface, understand how users think and talk about their domain.
2. Discovery is not optional — it is the prerequisite to all design work.
3. Skipping discovery is not faster. It produces rework.

## Use the User's Language

Terminology in software must match the language users already use in their day-to-day work.

1. Status names, action labels, field names, and navigation items should feel immediately intuitive to the target user.
2. If a user has to be trained on what a status or label means, the naming has failed.
3. Names are not an internal decision — they are a product decision that requires user input.

**Anti-pattern**: TowFlow uses barge statuses called "Pending," "Archive," and "Closed." These labels were defined by an internal stakeholder and are not intuitive to actual operators. Users do not understand what the statuses mean or how to use them correctly.

## Lead with Expertise

User research and discovery are inputs, not outputs. The product team's job is to synthesize them into opinionated design proposals.

1. Users describe problems, not solutions. Do not ask users to design the product — they cannot.
2. The product team must deeply understand the ICP (ideal customer profile) and their day-to-day workflows.
3. Propose thoughtful, opinionated solutions based on domain expertise. Do not wait for users to imagine what the software should look like.
4. Show prototypes and mockups — lead the conversation with informed suggestions and let users react.
5. Guide the user; do not defer to them on design decisions.
6. Being "user-centered" means understanding and solving user problems, not outsourcing design to users.
